# Hello world

Inspired by the timeline I shared at the [fireside chat](https://twitter.com/turingway/status/1538893924022259712) organised by The Turing Way, the first entry of my blog is a reflection on what my journey over the last 11 years has looked like.

I'll briefly recount my most relevant experiences in open environmental science and information technologies through a series of anecdotes, organised by stages. 
These anecdotes go beyond the technical and include opportunities I'm sharing so that others on similar paths can take advantage of them.

## Let's begin!

### Stage 0: beginnings, < 2011

Although I couldn't capture it in my timeline, my first encounters with  spatial information technologies came during my undergraduate studies in Agronomic Engineering. 
I had the opportunity to take modules on spatial models and data as part of the Geomatics postgraduate programme at my alma mater, Universidad Nacional de Colombia. 
I also went on an exchange to the University of São Paulo, Brazil, where I got to know a new culture and joined research groups using geoprocessing technologies applied to environmental and agricultural studies. 
During this exchange I trained in several satellite image-processing tools, both closed-license ones such as ERDAS and ENVI and open-license ones such as SPRING. 
My GIS skills were mainly in ArcGIS, with a little QGIS. Towards the end of my studies, in May 2011, I had the chance to put my knowledge to the test with my first consultancy, producing a database and cartography for a catalogue of medicinal plants in Colombia (see details
[here](../../projects.md#unal-aromatic)).

```{admonition} Opportunity 1 - International mobility
:class: tip
Did you know that most undergraduate programmes have exchange agreements with international universities? For example, the International Relations Office ([ORI](http://ori.bogota.unal.edu.co/)) of the Universidad Nacional de Colombia holds a series of agreements with universities worldwide. 
Thanks to these agreements, I was able to spend 6 months in Brazil, and I also received support to attend the XV Brazilian Remote Sensing Symposium, where I presented research results from one of the groups I was involved with (see the paper [here](http://marte.sid.inpe.br/col/dpi.inpe.br/marte/2011/07.13.13.19/doc/p1386.pdf)).
If you're an undergraduate or postgraduate student, I encourage you to look into these programmes — they often go unnoticed, yet they're hugely beneficial for experiencing other cultures and other ways of doing science.
```

### Stage 1: open deforestation data, 2011-2014

As part of my undergraduate professional placement I joined the [Terra-i](http://www.terra-i.org/) project at the International Center for Tropical Agriculture (CIAT), now known as the [Alliance of Bioversity International and CIAT](https://alliancebioversityciat.org).
This project had a very positive impact on my professional development. 
Terra-i is an initiative for global habitat-loss monitoring, generating deforestation alerts freely and openly. Given the project's relevance to the organisation's agenda, I took part in several sub-projects that used the tool's data to quantify the state of ecosystems in Latin America and the Caribbean, as well as to forecast the impact of road infrastructure projects in the region (see details [here](../../projects.md#ciat-terrai)).

As a proactive researcher I represented the project at several international events on conservation, forests and geospatial technologies. 
I also extensively promoted the use of the open data through case studies on the project blog (see, for [example](http://www.terra-i.org/news/news/Is-the--Paraguayan-Gran-Chaco--at-risk-for-extreme-habitat-destruction-.html)).
I authored and coordinated a protocol for field validation of deforestation alerts in the Peruvian Amazon, which was later replicated in other regions.
In terms of communities, I was a scholar of the [Society for Conservation GIS (SCGIS)](https://scgis.org/about), a programme funded by ESRI and environmental organisations, which I personally recommend for improving GIS skills and meeting other researchers around the world applying these technologies to conservation.

```{admonition} Opportunity 2 - GIS training programmes
:class: tip
As I mentioned above, I had the opportunity to be a scholar of the SCGIS programme. Applications open annually, selecting professionals working in conservation to improve their skills in GIS tools, mainly ArcGIS (see details [here](https://scgis.org/training)). The programme also enables scholars to present their work at the [ESRI User Conference](https://www.esri.com/en-us/about/events/uc/overview) and at the [annual SCGIS conference](https://scgis.org/conference). 
The process is competitive, but with good motivation, preparation and evidence of your conservation work in the application form, it's possible to be selected.
It's worth noting that the programme only partially covers costs, so you'll need external support for certain expenses such as flights and, if required, a visa. 
Other training programmes and summer schools can be found on topics beyond conservation. My advice is to look for those you have the greatest affinity with, and which offer the chance to stay connected with the instructors and colleagues in your cohort.
```

### Stage 2: MSc and PhD in Geography, 2014-2020

Through the Terra-i project I met [Mark Mulligan](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/people/mark-mulligan), a professor at King's College London, with whom — in collaboration with the Terra-i project — we tackled the study of the drivers of deforestation in the Amazon biome. 
It was a period in which I trained in and applied data science, artificial intelligence and cloud computing. 
In my MSc I analysed the spatial patterns of deforestation using data mining and fractal analysis (see details [here](../../projects.md#kcl-master)). 
Using two open deforestation data sources, Terra-i and the [Global Forest Change](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1244693) maps, my research managed to map the spatial distribution of patterns associated with different agents of deforestation. 
In my PhD I took a deeper approach, applying time-series analysis techniques to extract information on land cover and land use following deforestation from satellite imagery (see details [here](../../projects.md#kcl-phd)). 
This research pioneered the use of convolutional recurrent neural networks for classifying MODIS image time series. 
The models were trained using the Terra-i project's hardware resources as well as Google cloud-computing credits.

Beyond the technical progress during my studies, I think the most exciting part was maximising collaboration and mobility (exchanges, placements, conferences and research groups). 
For instance, during my MSc I did a placement at [Global Canopy](https://globalcanopy.org/), an environmental NGO based in Oxford, United Kingdom, where I carried out an analysis of water security in the Amazon (see details [here](../../projects.md#gcp-water)).
During my PhD I went on exchanges to HEIG-VD in Switzerland and later, in 2019, to CIAT's regional office in Asia, in Vietnam. 
At the first institution I trained in Python programming, Big Data and Deep Learning in the lab led by Professor [Andres Perez-Uribe](http://www.alunaweb.net/andres/), and at the second I refined my analyses and models with my co-supervisor at CIAT, [Dr Louis Reymondin](https://ciat.cgiar.org/ciat-coffee-cocoa-portfolio/louis-reymondin/).
I'd also highlight the internship in the summer of 2018 at the [Satellite Applications Catapult](https://sa.catapult.org.uk/) on the Harwell Science and Innovation Campus (United Kingdom), where I took part in the [Frontier Development Lab (FDL)](https://frontierdevelopmentlab.org/) accelerator. 
There, alongside other researchers affiliated with European institutions, I worked on mapping informal settlements from medium-resolution imagery such as Sentinel-2 and very-high-resolution imagery such as WorldView-4 (see details [here](../../projects.md#fdl-illegalsettlements)).

```{admonition} Opportunity 3 - Free research credits for Google and Microsoft cloud computing
:class: tip
In addition to the support from the Terra-i project's hardware resources, I took the initiative to explore cloud computing through the education programmes run by Google and Microsoft. 
Google offers a [research credits programme](https://edu.google.com/intl/ALL_us/programs/credits/research).
Microsoft, for its part, runs the AI for Earth programme, which sponsors Azure platform credits for environment-related research. 
Unlike Google's general-purpose programme, the [AI for Earth](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/ai/ai-for-earth-grants) credits go beyond providing the cloud service and aim to connect all the researchers and initiatives proposing data-science and AI-based methodologies to make an impact in their environmental projects.
I don't favour either programme — each has its advantages depending on the needs of your research and the community you want to belong to. Both require completing an application form with a series of questions about your research and an estimate of the total credits required. To maximise your chances of being selected, my suggestion is to make the most of each cloud provider's trial subscription. 
That way you can produce a better estimate of the credits required, as well as of additional services you can mention in the application.
```

## Summary

In this first instalment of Alejandro 101 I've briefly recounted my experiences from my undergraduate studies through to my PhD. 
Many different people inspired me, and I was fortunate to collaborate with them throughout those years. 
It was a period full of professional and personal achievements, with very valuable friendships I still keep, as well as the joy of starting a family during the third year of my PhD.

In the next instalment I'll continue with the second part of the timeline, from which I believe I'm successfully building new foundations for leadership in open environmental science. 
I'll also share opportunities and experiences that may prove interesting!
